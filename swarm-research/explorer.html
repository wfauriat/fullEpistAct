Why These Three Labs Exist

Every automated pipeline, statistical model, or human-AI workflow acts through a finite representation of the world. Standard evaluations focus on the producer's average error rate—how often the model or team gets routine cases right. Yet in real-world deployments, safety and decision quality depend on what escapes the frame and the checks before an irreversible action occurs.

Each of the three interactive tabs isolates one structural mechanism from Agir d’après une représentation du monde (Models M1, M2, and chain composition) and the cross-disciplinary synthesis. Rather than reading equations passively, you can perturb one assumption at a time and watch where intuition breaks.

Lab 1 — Model M1: VPP Collapse & Shared Models

Core question: If an AI tool cuts individual mistakes by 5×, why can total escaped risk still jump—or why do reviewers stop catching the mistakes that remain?

s = (1 − c)(1 − m)  |  VPP = P(E)s / [P(E)s + (1−P(E))f]  |  R = P(E)(1 − s·u)

What the sliders represent

  • Producer error rate P(E): How often the generator (human or LLM) produces a flawed claim.
  • Shared causes c: Fraction of errors driven by blind spots shared between producer and checker (same model family, overlapping training data, or shared framing). Even if the checker audits 100% of alerts (u = 1), at least c of errors pass invisibly.
  • Independent miss rate m & false-alarm rate f: How often the checker misses non-shared errors, versus crying wolf on valid outputs.
  • Uptake u: Behavioral probability matching (u = VPP, Bliss et al. 1995) vs. auditing every alert (u = 1).

Two "Aha!" experiments to try

  • Click Peer Review (10%, c=10%) then One Model for All (2%, c=60%): VPP plunges from 61.5% to 11.6% (8.7 alerts per true catch), and escape shoots to 96.3%—yet mean risk R still drops (5.57% → 1.93%) because P(E) fell fivefold.
  • Now click Shallow AI Gain (7%, c=60%): when shared-model monoculture raises c without a massive drop in P(E), residual risk R = 6.27% actually becomes worse than the unchecked 10% peer-review baseline! Also inspect the orange curve in the left plot to see the non-monotonic bump where lowering P(E) triggers VPP collapse.

Lab 2 — Serial Chains vs. Cross-Checks (N Steps)

Core question: What happens when we chain LLMs into N-step agentic pipelines (extract → summarize → plan → execute) instead of isolated single-turn answers?

Uncontrolled: 1 − (1 − p)ᴺ(1 − q)  |  Cross-checked (m = p): 1 − (1 − p²)ᴺ(1 − q)

What the sliders represent

  • Pipeline length N: Number of sequential handoffs or tool calls where any unrecovered error derails the run.
  • Local step error p: Per-step mistake probability (and miss rate m = p for an independent stepwise verifier).
  • Shared omitted factor q: Frame-level blind spot, environment shift, or interface laundering affecting the whole pipeline together.

What to watch in the chart

  • At N = 12, p = 6%, q = 3% (Ch. 14 preset), an unmonitored chain fails 53.8% of runs—even though each step is 94% reliable!
  • Adding independent stepwise cross-checks (blue curve) collapses local error propagation (p² = 0.36% per step), dropping total failures to 7.11%.
  • Notice the purple dashed floor q: once local steps are cross-checked, 42.2% of all remaining failures come from the shared factor q. Further local polishing cannot cross that floor—only changing the frame or breaking shared assumptions can.

Lab 3 — Model M2: One-Shot Stakes & Silent Traps

Core question: How should you decide on a high-stakes, one-shot action when an omitted factor z may exist outside your model—and what happens when stress couples p and z together?

p* = 1 / (1 + L/ΔU)  |  Act iff p + b < p*  |  p_w ∈ [p+z−pz, min(1, p+z)]

What the sliders represent

  • Loss asymmetry L / ΔU: Ratio of catastrophic loss to safe upside. Under Savage's expected utility, you should act only if real failure risk is below p*. At 10,000×, p* ≈ 0.01%—far smaller than finite data can reliably estimate.
  • Modeled risk p vs. omitted risk z: Your model estimates p, while reality also carries unmodeled risk z.
  • Humility margin b: An explicit prudence buffer added outside the model (p + b < p*).
  • Stress coupling (0% → 100%): At 0%, p and z fail independently; toward 100%, common stress pushes real-world risk pw up to the Fréchet–Hoeffding union bound min(1, p + z).

The four M2 regimes

  • Justified Departure: Both rule and reality stay safely below p*.
  • Silent Trap (red): Your model + margin says "go" (p + b < p*), yet omitted z pushes real risk above p*.
  • Saved by Margin (green): Naive model p wanted to go, but margin b caught the trap in time.
  • Superfluous Caution (amber): Margin b blocked a bet that would have been worth taking—the visible cost of prudence.

What These Three Interactive Labs Deliberately Leave Out

Consistent with the monograph's Conclusion and our cross-domain synthesis, toy sliders make mechanisms visible without claiming to capture full operational reality:

  • No formula sets the humility margin b or discovers z: An omitted variable z that stayed constant during data collection leaves zero trace in historical variance. Discovering it requires external contact (testing in a reversible sandbox or an independent observer), not adjusting sliders inside the model.
  • Uptake u and bypass are behavioral, not fixed constants: In Lab 1, raising friction to force u = 1 works in equations, yet in live organizations uniform Cognitive Forcing Functions lower user satisfaction (Buçinca et al. 2021) and can push staff toward unmonitored Shadow AI—motivating risk-tiered checks and deterministic capability locks (CaMeL / VeriGuard) rather than blanket manual checklists.
  • Illustrative numbers ≠ field calibration: Presets reproduce Annex D of Agir d’après une représentation du monde to expose sign flips and phase boundaries; measuring true c, m, f, and u in a real team requires blinded fault-injection benchmarks (erreurs injectées).
