# Field 6: Software Engineering & Formal Verification

## Executive Framing

Classical software engineering separates *what* a component must do (specification) from *how* it does it (implementation). Machine learning (ML) and agentic LLMs disrupt this boundary: behavior is induced from data, loss functions, and preference tuning rather than deduced from explicit contracts, and multi-step agents synthesize control flow while mutating external state.

The core tension is **verification versus validation**. Formal tools—type systems, SMT solvers, proof assistants, runtime shields, and capability interpreters—can increasingly *verify* conformance to a formal specification, even with an untrusted neural component inside the loop. Yet formal verification cannot *validate* that the specification faithfully captures real-world intent, dynamics, or unenumerated edge cases. Modern software engineering for AI is therefore an architectural discipline for **partitioning trust boundaries**, **containing blast radii**, and **exposing semantic mismatches** before irreversible actions occur.

## 1. Specified vs. Learned Components

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled

* **Contractual modularity in classical software:** Classical software reliability relies on explicit interfaces—preconditions, postconditions, and invariants (Hoare 1969; Meyer 1992)—combined with static type checking, modular abstraction boundaries, and localizable failure addresses. When a contract is violated, blame can be assigned to either the caller (precondition failure) or the callee (postcondition failure).
* **Entanglement and the CACE principle in ML:** Machine learning shifts the effective specification into training data distributions, feature pipelines, loss functions, and preference tuning. Sculley et al. (2015) established that ML systems accumulate "hidden technical debt" characterized by boundary erosion and the **CACE principle (*Changing Anything Changes Everything*)**: altering a feature, sampling rule, hyperparameter, or upstream data source can propagate non-local behavioral changes across the entire system, breaking classical modular isolation.
* **The specification gap for perception and open-world tasks:** Seshia et al. (*Toward Verified Artificial Intelligence*) identify the *specification gap* as a structural barrier: formal verification requires crisp logical or temporal specifications (e.g., Linear or Signal Temporal Logic), whereas high-dimensional perceptual or linguistic tasks (e.g., mapping $\sim 10^6$ pixels to "pedestrian" or natural language to "helpful and safe") resist closed-form mathematical characterization.
* **The historical critique of pure formalism:** Well before modern deep learning, DeMillo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) argued in *"Social Processes and Proofs of Theorems and Programs"* that formal proofs are only as trustworthy as the informal-to-formal translation of the specification itself. Modern **specification gaming** (cataloged by Krakovna et al.) operationalizes this critique: optimization algorithms act as literal-minded adversaries that satisfy the formal proxy while violating unstated human intent.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **Can ML components be given meaningful modular contracts?**
  * *Probabilistic/statistical contract school:* Argues that learned modules should be integrated via distributional contracts—specifying calibration bounds, out-of-distribution (OOD) abstention thresholds, or worst-case perturbation envelopes—allowing compositional reasoning across hybrid systems.
  * *Systems-skeptic / empirical school:* Contends that because real-world distribution shifts violate the i.i.d. or bounded-perturbation assumptions underlying statistical contracts, treating a neural network as a contract-bearing module creates an *illusion of modularity*. From this view, reliability must be engineered at the closed-loop system level via continuous monitoring, shadowing, and rollback rather than component-level proofs.
* **Where should the deterministic–learned boundary be drawn?**
  * *Thin-neural / thick-symbolic architectures* restrict learned models to narrow perception, ranking, or candidate-proposal roles while keeping state transitions, authorization, and invariants in deterministic code.
  * *End-to-end learned / agentic architectures* argue that brittle hand-coded symbolic boundaries bottleneck capability on messy, unstructured tasks and merely shift failure modes into the rigid symbolic glue. How to optimize this boundary as foundation model capabilities shift remains unsettled.
* **Specification mining vs. manual specification:** Seshia et al. and subsequent work advocate *specification mining*—inferring formal properties from demonstrations and simulation traces—to avoid manual authoring bottlenecks. Critics note that mined specifications inherit the blind spots and biases of the training traces, risking circularity when used to verify systems trained on similar data.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Empirical Tests (and Known Limitations)

| Pattern / Framework | Mechanism | Known Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Narrow-waist / typed interfaces** | Constrain learned outputs to typed schemas, enums, or domain-specific intermediate representations checked by deterministic parsers/validators before state mutation. | Enforces syntactic and structural validity, not semantic truth; a schema-valid JSON payload can still encode a hallucinated or harmful action. |
| **Specification mining & scenario-based falsification** (*Scenic*, *VerifAI*; Seshia et al.) | Probabilistic programming languages specify structured environmental scenarios to systematically search for counterexamples and mine temporal logic specs in simulation. | Bound by simulator fidelity (*sim-to-real gap*); omitted environmental variables never appear in falsification searches. |
| **Verification condition languages** (*Vehicle*) | Bridge the **embedding gap** between continuous neural spaces and discrete symbolic logic by compiling unified neuro-symbolic properties to both neural verifiers ($\alpha\beta$-CROWN, Marabou) and theorem provers/SMT solvers (Lean, Dafny, Z3). | Neural network verification scales poorly to frontier-scale models (billions of parameters) and typically certifies only local $L_p$-norm robustness, not semantic invariance. |
| **Shadow deployment, canarying & metamorphic testing** | Run learned components alongside deterministic baselines or test invariance under semantics-preserving input transformations without requiring an oracle. | Detects regressions only on observed traffic distributions; blind to rare, high-consequence tail events that do not occur during shadow windows. |

## 2. Runtime Monitors, Simplex & Autoformalization

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled

* **Runtime assurance decouples performance from safety certification:** In the **Simplex Architecture** (Sha 2001) and its modern descendants (e.g., *SOTER*, runtime shielding), a high-performance, unverified controller (such as a deep RL policy) runs inside a formally verified envelope. A trusted decision module monitors system state and switches control to a simpler, formally verified backup controller whenever the primary controller threatens to exit the recoverable safety region.
* **Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) enable minimally invasive shielding:** Rather than coarse binary switching, CBF-based safety filters solve a real-time Quadratic Program (QP) to minimally perturb a learned controller's action so as to maintain forward invariance of a formally defined safe set.
* **Proof assistants provide machine-checkable internal soundness:** Interactive theorem provers such as **Lean 4** (Moura & Ullrich 2021) reduce trust to a small, audited kernel. **Autoformalization**—using LLMs to translate informal mathematics or specifications into formal code (Wu et al. 2022; followed in 2024–2026 by systems such as *AlphaProof*, *DeepSeek-Prover*, *FormL4*, and *Leanstral*)—demonstrates that LLMs can leverage compiler/kernel feedback loops to produce machine-verified proofs at rapidly increasing rates.
* **Compilation is necessary, not sufficient:** Across 2024–2026 autoformalization research, a clear consensus has emerged: Lean kernel acceptance certifies *internal logical validity* of the proof relative to the formalized statement, but does **not** certify *semantic fidelity* between the informal human claim and the formal Lean statement.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **Closing the semantic gap in autoformalization:** Recent analyses highlight severe failure modes in LLM autoformalization:
  * *Implicit hypothesis alteration:* Dropping boundary conditions, silently changing variable domains (e.g., reals to integers), or altering quantifier dependencies.
  * *Proof-driven statement distortion:* Because RL-trained provers (e.g., via GRPO) are rewarded for compiling proofs, models can subtly weaken or rewrite the theorem statement into a trivial variant that compiles cleanly while leaving the original informal claim uncertified.
  * *Paraphrase fragility:* Semantically invariant rephrasings of an informal problem still cause sharp drops in compilation and alignment accuracy, indicating reliance on surface patterns rather than deep semantic parsing.
  * Whether automated round-trip auditing can reliably bound this gap—or whether human expert inspection of formal statements remains an irreducible bottleneck ("nobody has to look at the proof, but *someone* still has to validate the statement")—is actively debated.
* **Analytical vs. learned safety monitors:** Classical Simplex and CBF monitors require an accurate analytical model of system dynamics. Because learned controllers are often deployed precisely where dynamics are poorly modeled, recent work (2023–2025) attempts to *learn* barrier functions or monitors from data. Critics point out a **circular dependency**: supervising an unverified learned controller with an unverified learned monitor reintroduces OOD failure modes into the safety layer itself, undermining the very guarantee the wrapper was meant to provide.
* **Safety vs. liveness/performance under conservative shields:** Heavy-handed monitors preserve safety by frequently overriding the learned component, collapsing system behavior to the low-performance backup controller or causing physical *chattering* (high-frequency oscillation at the switching boundary). How to make shields tight enough to preserve utility without eroding safety margins remains an open control and SE problem.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Empirical Tests (and Known Limitations)

* **Cycle-Consistency Fine-Tuning & Decomposition-Guided Auditing (2025–2026):** Newer autoformalization pipelines enforce round-trip validation ($\text{NL} \to \text{Lean 4} \to \text{NL}'$) using embedding similarity or RL rewards to penalize semantic drift, alongside multi-step decomposition and error-taxonomy benchmarks (e.g., ICML *FormalRx*). *Limitation:* Round-trip translation can suffer from symmetric blind spots—if both the formalizer and back-translator share the same LLM misconceptions or smooth over the same subtle boundary condition, semantic distortion passes undetected.
* **Process-Driven Autoformalization (*FormL4*, 2024–2025) & Agentic Proof Checking (*Lean4Agent*):** Step-by-step compiler feedback improves syntactic well-formedness, and dependent types in Lean 4 are being explored to verify agentic workflow invariants. *Limitation:* High authoring/formalization overhead limits applicability outside domains with crisp mathematical or typed-protocol semantics.
* **CBF Safety Filters & Simplex Implementations:** Empirically effective in robotics and cyber-physical benchmarks with known low-dimensional dynamics. *Known failure modes (2023–2025 literature):*
  1. *Model mismatch:* Unmodeled friction, payload drift, or environmental perturbations invalidate forward-invariance proofs.
  2. *QP infeasibility:* Safety constraints can conflict with physical actuator saturation limits, leaving no feasible safe control input.
  3. *High relative degree & myopia:* Constructing CBFs when inputs only affect higher derivatives is non-trivial, and myopic one-step invariance can steer systems into inescapable "dead-end" states at $t+k$.
  4. *Latency & perception coupling:* Inference and hypervisor serialization delays between rich ML OS domains and real-time safety domains can cause late interventions, and monitors relying on raw visual sensors inherit perception vulnerabilities.

## 3. Agentic Pipelines vs. Deterministic Orchestration

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled

* **Serial error compounding and state mutation:** When an LLM agent executes an $N$-step tool-calling loop, per-step error probabilities compound serially across planning, tool-argument synthesis, and observation parsing. Unlike stateless text generation, agentic loops mutate external state (files, databases, APIs, financial ledgers), turning transient reasoning errors into persistent side effects.
* **Control-flow / data-flow conflation is a structural vulnerability:** Traditional LLM agents concatenate trusted system/user instructions and untrusted external data (emails, web pages, tool outputs) into a single token stream. Security research treats this as structurally analogous to SQL injection: prompt-level defenses and classifier guardrails devolve into an unwinnable "whack-a-mole" game against adaptive prompt injections.
* **Defense-in-depth requires architectural isolation:** There is broad engineering consensus that safety and security for production agents cannot rely solely on model alignment or prompt engineering; deterministic runtime enforcement—sandboxing, least-privilege capabilities, explicit policy checks, and deterministic workflow boundaries—must constrain what the model *can* do regardless of what it outputs.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **Free-form agentic loops vs. deterministic state-machine orchestration:**
  * *Deterministic-orchestration school:* Keeps control flow, branching, retries, and state transitions in explicit deterministic code (workflows, DAGs, state machines), invoking LLMs strictly as bounded, typed subroutines at specific nodes. This restores debuggability, idempotency, and auditability.
  * *Autonomous-agent school:* Argues that pre-scripting control graphs fails on open-ended tasks (e.g., novel debugging, multi-source research) where the necessary tool sequence cannot be anticipated upfront; restricting autonomy sacrifices the core value proposition of LLM agents.
* **Capability-based isolation vs. utility loss:** Architectures like the **Dual LLM** pattern and **CaMeL** strictly separate privileged planning from quarantined data parsing. A central unresolved tension is that many legitimate tasks *require* untrusted data to influence control flow (e.g., reading an email to decide *which* calendar event to reschedule or *whom* to reply to). Overly strict taint policies block legitimate workflows or force frequent human approvals (degrading usability), while permissive escape hatches reopen injection vectors.
* **Symbolic/deterministic policy enforcement vs. LLM-as-judge guardrails:** Using a second LLM to audit a primary agent's actions offers semantic flexibility over natural-language policies, but introduces latency, non-determinism, and correlated failure modes (shared prompt-injection susceptibility or shared blind spots). Deterministic DSLs and symbolic policy verifiers offer millisecond-scale guarantees and zero LLM-inference variance in the hot path, but struggle to express fuzzy, context-dependent policies (e.g., "do not send an email that could embarrass the company").

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Empirical Tests (and Known Limitations)

* **Dual LLM Pattern & CaMeL (*Capabilities for Machine Learning*, Google DeepMind / ETH Zurich, 2025):** Formalized in *"Defeating Prompt Injections by Design"*, CaMeL separates a *Privileged LLM* (which plans from trusted user intent without seeing raw untrusted data) from a *Quarantined LLM* (which parses untrusted data into structured values without tool privileges). A custom Python-like interpreter tracks data provenance via **capability metadata and taint tags**, enforcing fine-grained security policies on tool calls. Evaluated on *AgentDojo*, CaMeL sharply reduces prompt-injection success rates. *Limitations:* Cannot prevent data-channel smuggling when a quarantined model is tricked into mapping malicious content into a benign-looking structured field that legitimately influences downstream actions; requires engineering explicit security policies per tool and domain.
* **Hard Execution Sandboxing (*IsolateGPT*, NDSS; micro-VMs / containers):** Isolates tool execution and code interpreters inside ephemeral, zero-trust sandboxes with restricted filesystem, network, and credential scopes so that a hijacked agent's blast radius is physically bounded. *Limitations:* Protects host infrastructure and unrelated secrets, but does not prevent misuse of the *legitimate* credentials or APIs the agent was intentionally granted to perform its task (the "confused deputy" problem).
* **Runtime Verification DSLs (*AgentSpec*, 2025–2026):** Provides a lightweight domain-specific language of *Triggers*, *Predicates*, and *Enforcements* (halt, pause for human approval, force replanning) hooked into agent loops (e.g., LangChain) across coding, robotics, and driving stacks. *Limitations:* Rule coverage is bounded by what engineers (or rule-synthesizing LLMs) anticipate; rules that are too broad cause false-positive friction, while narrow rules miss novel failure modes.
* **Dual-Stage Offline-Verified / Online-Monitored Guards (*VeriGuard*, 2025):** Separates heavy symbolic formal verification of behavioral policies into an *offline* stage, leaving a lightweight, millisecond-scale deterministic monitor *online* without LLM inference in the critical path. *Limitations:* Policies must be formalizable offline; unexpected shifts in task intent or tool semantics during deployment can render pre-verified policies stale or overly restrictive.
* **Code-Generating Guardrail Agents (*GuardAgent*, 2024–2025):** Uses a reasoning LLM with memory to translate natural-language safety requests into executable guardrail code, evaluated on healthcare access control (*EICU-AC*) and web navigation (*Mind2Web-SC*). *Limitations:* Shifts trust to the guardrail code-generation step; erroneous translations can fail open (missed violations) or fail closed (broken legitimate tasks).
* **Transactional Boundaries, Idempotency & Rollback Patterns:** Production agentic engineering increasingly borrows distributed-systems primitives—dry-run/staging environments, idempotency keys, compensating transactions (Saga-style rollbacks), and human-in-the-loop commit gates for irreversible external mutations (e.g., sending money, deleting records, pushing to production). *Limitations:* Many real-world side effects (sending an email, leaking confidential data over a network, physical actuation) are fundamentally non-rollbackable, forcing reliance on pre-commit capability controls rather than post-hoc undo.

## Cross-Domain Interactions & Interfaces

* **Epistemology & Frame Problem (Field 1):** The *specification gap* and *semantic gap* instantiate open-background rules and the frame problem (McCarthy & Hayes 1969; Dennett 1984; Shanahan 2016). Formal verification checks internal consistency (Oberkampf & Roy 2010), while DeMillo, Lipton, and Perlis (1979) echo Mayo's *severe testing* (1996, 2018) and Wimsatt's *robustness analysis* (1981): confidence in a spec requires surviving diverse external challenges, not circular self-certification.
* **Bayesian Probability & Misspecification (Field 2):** Formal specs and CBF dynamics define $\mathcal{M}$-closed worlds; deployed learned systems are $\mathcal{M}$-open. When true dynamics or intent lie outside the formalized class, proofs lose external validity just as misspecified posteriors concentrate on wrong parameters.
* **Uncertainty Quantification & Robustness (Field 3):** Conformal prediction (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023) and Robust Decision Making (Lempert, Popper & Bankes 2003) can dynamically widen Simplex/CBF safety margins as residuals grow, though both remain blind to omitted variables constant during calibration.
* **Decision Theory (Field 4):** When catastrophic loss $L \gg \Delta U$ drives the expected-utility threshold $p^* = \Delta U / (\Delta U + L) \to 0$, statistical ML estimates cannot reliably separate $p < p^*$ from $p > p^*$. Deterministic capability controls, sandboxes, and pre-commit locks serve as structural safeguards where probabilistic estimation fails.
* **Risk Engineering & Safety Science (Field 5):** Common-cause failure (Knight & Leveson 1986) threatens LLM-as-judge guardrails and round-trip autoformalization when producer and verifier share model families. *Alert fatigue* (Bliss, Gilson & Deaton 1995) mirrors over-conservative shields and noisy approval gates. *Interface laundering* (Mars Climate Orbiter 1999; Knight Capital 2012) recurs whenever uncertainty or taint provenance is stripped at the neural–symbolic boundary.
* **ML & LLM Behavior (Field 7):** "Jagged frontiers" (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023) complicate capability scoping, and unavoidable hallucinations on rare facts (Kalai & Vempala 2024; Kalai et al. 2025) motivate deterministic verification over prompting alone. *Proof-driven statement distortion* in RL-trained autoformalizers exemplifies Goodhart's Law (1975) on compiler rewards.
* **Information Economics (Field 8):** Formal proofs (Lean 4) and deterministic execution traces could restore **costly signaling** (Spence 1973) after generative AI collapses the cost of polished prose (Akerlof 1970)—but an unvalidated formal statement turns a "Lean-verified" badge into a misleading veneer.
* **Human Factors & Oversight (Field 9):** Bainbridge's (1983) *ironies of automation* leave humans with the hardest task—auditing subtle formal specs and rare escalations—while eroding hands-on mental models. The 2025 METR developer trial (Becker et al. 2025) shows perceived AI velocity can invert objective gains without structured verification friction (e.g., pre-mortems, Klein 2007).

## High-Leverage Leads & Open Questions

1. **Auditable Statement Validation for Autoformalization:** Beyond round-trip translation (which can share correlated blind spots), can independent, non-LLM or multi-paradigm checks—such as automated property-based counterexample generation against informal test cases, or structured human-readable diffing of implicit hypotheses—provide *severe tests* of formal statement fidelity?
2. **Composable Provenance & Uncertainty Across the Neural–Symbolic Boundary:** How can systems propagate *both* security taint (as in CaMeL) and calibrated epistemic uncertainty (e.g., conformal prediction intervals or semantic entropy) across typed interfaces without interface laundering, so deterministic orchestrators can dynamically tighten capability scopes when model uncertainty spikes?
3. **Principled Escape Hatches in Capability-Controlled Agents:** In architectures like CaMeL and Dual LLM, what minimal, formally auditable patterns allow untrusted data to safely parameterize control flow without collapsing into either unusable rigidity or wide-open prompt injection?
4. **Breaking the Circularity of Learned Runtime Monitors:** When exact analytical dynamics are unavailable for CBFs/Simplex, can hybrid monitors combine coarse, provably conservative physical/protocol invariants (verified offline, *VeriGuard*-style) with online OOD detectors whose failure mode defaults safely to human escalation or graceful degradation?
5. **Empirical Escape-Probability Measurement for Agentic Guardrails:** Borrowing from mutation testing (DeMillo, Lipton & Sayward 1978) and fault injection, what standardized adversarial-mutation benchmarks can measure the *true escape probability* of runtime monitors (AgentSpec, VeriGuard, GuardAgent, CaMeL) under adaptive attackers and non-stationary real-world tool environments—rather than static benchmark suites?
