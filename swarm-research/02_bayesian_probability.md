# Field 2: Bayesian Statistics & Foundations of Probability

This survey examines four foundational pillars of probability and statistical inference—interpretations of probability, calibration and scoring rules, model misspecification ($\mathcal{M}$-open settings), and inductive generalization under distribution shift—through an open, non-presupposing lens. For each topic, we distinguish what is settled from active disputes, and examine concrete frameworks alongside their known limitations.

---

## 1. Frequentist, Objective Bayesian, and Subjective Bayesian Interpretations (and Single-Shot Events)

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
* **Shared calculus, divergent semantics:** Mainstream schools agree on Kolmogorov's probability axioms (or finitely additive betting variants); the dispute concerns what probability *refers to* and what warrants assigning a number to an event.
* **Coherence and representation theorems:** Within subjective Bayesianism (Savage 1954; de Finetti 1974; Lindley 2006), an agent whose preferences or betting odds satisfy coherence axioms acts *as if* holding a unique subjective probability measure updated by Bayes' rule. De Finetti's representation theorem further shows that infinite exchangeability of observables is mathematically equivalent to conditional independence given a latent distribution, allowing subjectivists to use parametric models without positing objective physical chances.
* **Asymptotic agreement under regular, well-specified models:** By the classical Bernstein–von Mises (BvM) theorem, when a finite-dimensional parametric model is well-specified and regular and the prior puts positive density near the truth, Bayesian posteriors converge to the same normal distribution as the frequentist MLE, aligning credible sets with frequentist confidence sets (see Kleijn & van der Vaart 2012 for where this breaks).

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved
* **Single-shot events and the reference-class problem:** Frequentism defines probability via long-run frequencies or procedural error rates across hypothetical repetitions. For genuinely unique, single-shot events (e.g., a novel engineering failure or one-off geopolitical shock), no natural collective exists; choosing a reference class is an unformalized judgment. Subjectivists (de Finetti 1974; Lindley 2006) take this as decisive evidence that all probabilities are personal degrees of belief. Frequentists and error statisticians counter that internal coherence does not manufacture external evidential warrant.
* **Objective/logical Bayesianism vs. parameterization dependence:** Logical/objective Bayesians (Jaynes 2003) seek unique, observer-independent priors encoding "states of knowledge" via Maximum Entropy or transformation-group invariance. Critics argue that "uninformative" priors fail to generalize cleanly to complex, high-dimensional, or non-symmetric spaces, can be improper or cause marginalization paradoxes, and remain sensitive to parameterization choices.
* **The Likelihood Principle vs. error probabilities (stopping rules):** A sharp divide separates Bayesians/likelihoodists from Deborah Mayo's *error-statistical* (severe testing) program (Mayo 1996, 2018). Under the Likelihood Principle, inference depends only on the likelihood of the observed data; stopping rules, data-dredging, and unperformed experiments are irrelevant. Error statisticians counter that ignoring *how* data were gathered destroys error control: without evaluating a procedure's counterfactual error probabilities, one cannot claim a hypothesis survived a *severe test* capable of exposing falsehoods.
* **Applied Bayesianism as falsificationism:** Gelman & Shalizi (2013) reject textbook subjective-Bayesian philosophy as a poor description of successful practice. Applied Bayesian analysis operates not by passive updating of a true subjective prior, but through an iterative hypothetico-deductive loop: **model building $\to$ posterior inference $\to$ predictive model checking (falsification)**. Posterior updating is internal deduction within a tentative model; checking the model against data is fundamentally non-Bayesian and aligns with error-statistical falsification.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Known Limitations
* **Imprecise probabilities (Walley 1991):** Represents severe uncertainty via sets of probability measures (lower/upper previsions) rather than a single sharp prior. *Limitation:* Intervals can dilate under conditioning, high-dimensional computation is intractable, and decision rules over credal sets remain contested.
* **Confidence distributions (Xie & Singh 2013):** Summarizes frequentist confidence intervals across all levels as a sample-dependent distribution function, yielding Bayesian-like summaries without priors. *Limitation:* Not true posterior probability distributions; can behave awkwardly under non-linear transformations or complex nuisance parameters.
* **Game-theoretic probability & Safe Anytime-Valid Inference (SAVI) (Shafer & Vovk; Ramdas, Grünwald, Vovk & Shafer 2023):** Frames testing as a sequential betting game using non-negative test martingales (**e-values**) and **confidence sequences**. E-values permit continuous monitoring and optional stopping without inflating Type I error, partially dissolving the Bayesian–frequentist stopping-rule dispute. *Limitation:* Anytime-validity incurs a modest power/width penalty versus fixed-sample optimal tests, and constructing sharp e-values for complex composite hypotheses remains active research.

## 2. Calibration & Proper Scoring Rules under Rare, Delayed, and Non-Stationary Outcomes

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
* **Strict propriety and truthfulness:** Gneiting & Raftery (2007) established the theory of **strictly proper scoring rules** (log score, Brier score, Continuous Ranked Probability Score [CRPS]): $\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim Q}[S(P, Y)]$ is uniquely optimized when the reported distribution $P$ equals the true distribution $Q$, incentivizing honest probabilistic reporting.
* **Sharpness subject to calibration:** Proper scores balance **calibration** (statistical compatibility between forecasts and realizations) and **sharpness** (concentration of predictive distributions). Calibration alone is trivial (e.g., predicting the historical base rate), while sharpness without calibration produces dangerous overconfidence.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved
* **The Forecaster's Dilemma on rare/extreme events:** Lerch, Thorarinsdottir, Ravazzolo & Gneiting (2017) proved a structural paradox: users care most about rare extremes (crashes, floods, catastrophic failures), yet evaluating forecasts *conditionally* on subsets where extreme events occurred (or multiplying a proper score by $\mathbb{I}(y > r)$) **destroys propriety**. Conditional evaluation rewards hedging and systematic over-forecasting ("crying wolf") over truthful calibration. Conversely, unconditional proper scoring over all outcomes is dominated by routine cases, yielding minimal statistical power to distinguish tail-risk skill.
* **Delayed feedback and non-stationarity:** Empirical calibration requires large samples from a stable regime. When outcomes are rare or long-delayed (multi-year safety, climate, or macro horizons), feedback arrives too slowly to discipline forecasters. Under non-stationarity, widening the historical window mixes incompatible regimes, whereas shrinking it leaves zero or near-zero extreme observations.
* **Proper score choice vs. downstream decision loss:** Strictly proper scores disagree sharply on misspecified models. Log score penalizes near-zero probability assigned to a realized surprise infinitely harshly (extreme tail sensitivity), whereas Brier score and CRPS are bounded and more forgiving of tail misses. Whether evaluation should use generic proper scores or asymmetric, decision-specific loss functions remains contested.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Known Limitations
* **Threshold- and quantile-weighted proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Ranjan 2011):** Rules such as threshold-weighted CRPS (*twCRPS*) apply continuous non-negative weights $w(y)$ across the predictive distribution rather than post-hoc data subsetting, preserving strict propriety while focusing on tails. *Limitation:* Still requires large unconditional samples for statistical power on rare events and upfront specification of $w(y)$.
* **Sequential e-value / martingale monitoring (Ramdas et al. 2023):** Evaluates calibration sequentially as a betting game against claimed probabilities, enabling continuous detection of degradation under non-stationarity. *Limitation:* Detecting subtle miscalibration on rare events still requires large event counts or risky leverage.
* **Conformal prediction (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023):** Provides distribution-free, finite-sample marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability without parametric assumptions. *Limitation:* Guarantees are *marginal* (not conditional on high-risk subgroups) and weaken under non-stationary distribution shift.

## 3. Model Misspecification ($\mathcal{M}$-Open Settings) & the Role of Subjective Priors

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
* **$\mathcal{M}$-closed vs. $\mathcal{M}$-open (Bernardo & Smith 1994):** Classical Bayesian theory assumes an **$\mathcal{M}$-closed** world where the true data-generating process $P^*$ lies inside the candidate model class $\mathcal{M}$. Practical science and engineering typically operate in **$\mathcal{M}$-open** worlds where $P^* \notin \mathcal{M}$.
* **Loss of uncertainty validity under misspecification (Kleijn & van der Vaart 2012):** Under parametric misspecification, the BvM posterior still concentrates around the Kullback–Leibler (KL) minimizer $\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P^* \| P_\theta)$, but its covariance no longer matches the true sampling ("sandwich") variance. Consequently, **Bayesian credible sets lose frequentist coverage validity** and can be arbitrarily overconfident even as $n \to \infty$.
* **Bayesian inconsistency:**
  * *Infinite-dimensional priors:* Diaconis & Freedman (1986) showed that natural nonparametric priors can yield inconsistent posteriors that fail to concentrate on the truth even with infinite data.
  * *Misspecified parametric models:* Grünwald & van Ommen (2017) proved that standard Bayesian inference, model selection, and Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) can be inconsistent even in simple misspecified linear models (e.g., homoscedastic likelihoods on heteroscedastic data). Via **hypercompression**, the posterior concentrates on overparameterized or poorly predicting models whose empirical KL fluctuations appear favorable in-sample.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved
* **Subjective priors in scarce-data regimes—regularization vs. blind spots:** Informative priors stabilize scarce-data inference by encoding domain constraints. Yet Bayes' rule is strictly multiplicative: hypotheses given zero prior mass (or omitted from $\Theta$) can never be recovered by data (violating Lindley's Cromwell's rule whenever unmodeled mechanisms exist). Where likelihoods are weak, strong priors dominate the posterior—silently laundering untested assumptions into crisp intervals.
* **BMA vs. predictive stacking:** In $\mathcal{M}$-open settings, BMA asymptotically collapses all weight onto the single candidate model closest to $P^*$ in KL divergence, even when a convex combination of models predicts strictly better (Yao et al. 2018). Whether to repair Bayesianism via richer hierarchical priors or adopt decision-theoretic predictive combination remains debated.
* **Coherence vs. $\mathcal{M}$-open robustness:** Modifying the likelihood (e.g., fractional power likelihoods) violates strict Dutch-book coherence. Pragmatists counter that coherence theorems presuppose an $\mathcal{M}$-closed space; exact updating inside a wrong model space merely guarantees coherent overconfidence.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Known Limitations
* **Generalized Bayes / Gibbs posteriors (Bissiri, Holmes & Walker 2016):** Gives an axiomatic decision-theoretic justification for loss-based belief updating with a learning rate $\eta$: $\pi_n(\theta) \propto \pi_0(\theta)\exp(-\eta \sum_i \ell(\theta, y_i))$, decoupling inference from assuming a true generative likelihood. *Limitation:* Calibrating $\eta$ is non-trivial and directly governs uncertainty width.
* **SafeBayes (Grünwald & van Ommen 2017):** Learns the power-likelihood rate $\eta \in (0, 1]$ empirically by minimizing sequential predictive risk, automatically dialing $\eta < 1$ when misspecification induces hypercompression. *Limitation:* Higher computational cost and sensitivity to the chosen predictive loss.
* **Bayesian stacking (Yao et al. 2018):** Optimizes convex combination weights of predictive distributions via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) under a proper score, bypassing the requirement that any candidate model be true. *Limitation:* Relies on exchangeability for LOO-CV and cannot extrapolate outside the candidate models' convex hull.
* **Martingale posteriors (Fong, Holmes & Walker 2023):** Replaces parameter priors and likelihoods with predictive sequences over missing/future observations (via Doob's martingale convergence theorem), deriving uncertainty on target functionals directly from imputed future populations. *Limitation:* Shifts misspecification risk to specifying high-dimensional predictive copulas/sequences.
* **Posterior predictive checking (Gelman & Shalizi 2013):** Tests replicated model data against observed data to expose structural misspecification. *Limitation:* Only catches discrepancies along test statistics the analyst thinks to check.

## 4. Inductive Generalization Beyond Bias–Variance and No Free Lunch

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
* **Operational meaning of No Free Lunch (Wolpert & Macready 1997):** Averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm beats random guessing out-of-sample. Induction succeeds *only* via **inductive bias** (smoothness, sparsity, symmetry, causal modularity) aligned with the real environment.
* **Double descent (Belkin):** Refutes classical monotonic U-shaped bias–variance trade-offs: beyond the interpolation threshold (zero training error), test error drops a *second* time in overparameterized regimes due to implicit regularization (e.g., minimum-norm interpolation).
* **Shortcut learning (Geirhos et al. 2020):** Deep networks trained via ERM routinely achieve high in-distribution accuracy by exploiting **shortcuts**—spurious, low-complexity correlations (textures, backgrounds, dataset artifacts) that collapse abruptly under distribution shift.

### (b) What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved
* **In-distribution success vs. abrupt OOD collapse:** The same simplicity/implicit-regularization biases that explain benign overfitting in-distribution cause models to prefer brittle shortcuts over causal features, producing sudden, unannounced failures out-of-distribution (OOD).
* **Causal invariance vs. DomainBed skepticism:** Invariant Causal Prediction (ICP; Peters, Bühlmann & co-authors 2016) shows that causal mechanisms remain invariant across interventions while spurious correlations shift, offering a theoretical path to OOD generalization. Yet rigorous benchmarking in **DomainBed** (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz 2020/2021) demonstrated that under fair model selection, standard ERM often matches or outperforms specialized invariance-based domain generalization methods. Whether this stems from immature algorithms, insufficient environment diversity, or fundamental identifiability limits remains unresolved.
* **PAC-Bayes under shift (Alquier 2024):** PAC-Bayes bridges Bayesian-style posteriors and frequentist risk via KL-regularized bounds, even achieving non-vacuous bounds for deep networks. However, standard guarantees assume i.i.d. (or known-bound) test distributions; non-vacuous guarantees under unanticipated structural shift remain open.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Frameworks & Known Limitations
* **PAC-Bayes bounds (Alquier 2024):** Bounds expected risk via empirical risk plus $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \| P)/n$. *Limitation:* Tightness requires informative priors $P$, and bounds break under unmodeled distribution shifts.
* **Invariant Causal Prediction (Peters et al. 2016) & multi-environment testing:** Filters out predictors whose conditional relationship with the target varies across environments. *Limitation:* Any spurious feature that stayed constant across *all* training environments is indistinguishable from a true causal invariant.
* **Runtime shift detection & abstention:** Monitors covariate or predictive-entropy shifts to trigger fallback. *Limitation:* High-dimensional detectors struggle to separate benign covariate drift from semantically fatal shifts without high false-alarm rates.

## 5. Cross-Domain Interactions & Interfaces

* **Field 1 (Epistemology & the Frame Problem):** The $\mathcal{M}$-open setting formalizes the frame problem (McCarthy & Hayes 1969; Shanahan 2016): Bayes' rule updates weights *inside* a fixed frame $\Theta$ but cannot invent omitted variables. Gelman & Shalizi (2013) and Mayo (2018) connect statistical model checking directly to non-circular falsification.
* **Field 3 (UQ & Robustness Analysis):** Kleijn & van der Vaart (2012) and Grünwald & van Ommen (2017) show why standard Bayesian intervals fail under structural uncertainty. Imprecise probabilities (Walley 1991), conformal prediction (Vovk et al. 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023), and generalized Bayes link statistical inference to RDM (Lempert et al. 2003). The ICP identifiability limit explains why omitted variables constant during observation leave zero historical variance signature.
* **Field 4 (Decision Theory):** When asymmetric one-shot stakes drive the Savage (1954) expected-utility threshold $p^* = \Delta U / (\Delta U + L)$ toward zero, the Forecaster's Dilemma (Lerch et al. 2017) and $\mathcal{M}$-open tail misspecification show probabilities near $p^*$ cannot be reliably calibrated—motivating non-probabilistic safeguards or operational locks.
* **Field 5 (Risk Engineering & Safety Science):** The Forecaster's Dilemma mirrors alert fatigue under low base rates (Bliss et al. 1995). Kleijn & van der Vaart's coverage breakdown exemplifies *interface laundering*: narrow in-model posterior intervals are passed downstream as objective failure-rate guarantees.
* **Field 6 (Software Engineering & Formal Verification):** Bayes' rule and PAC-Bayes offer internal *verification* conditional on model/i.i.d. assumptions, not external *validation* (Oberkampf & Roy 2010). SAVI/e-value monitors provide a formal bridge to runtime safety wrappers.
* **Field 7 (Machine Learning & LLM Behavior):** Proper scoring rules and calibration underpin analyses of unavoidable LLM hallucinations (Kalai & Vempala 2024; Kalai et al. 2025) and semantic-entropy detection (Farquhar et al. 2024). Shortcut learning (Geirhos et al. 2020) and $\mathcal{M}$-open overconfidence help explain jagged frontiers (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023) and sycophantic miscalibration (Sharma et al. 2024).
* **Field 8 (Information Economics & Collective Epistemology):** The Forecaster's Dilemma is an incentive-design trap where improper conditional metrics trigger Goodhart (1975) gaming. Shared misspecified priors across producers and reviewers induce correlated errors (shared-model monoculture), breaking ensemble independence.
* **Field 9 (Human Factors & AI-Assisted Research):** E-values / SAVI (Ramdas et al. 2023) accommodate iterative human peeking and AI-accelerated hypothesis generation without invalidating error control. Conversely, overly crisp misspecified posteriors suppress human uncertainty cues (Bainbridge 1983; Becker et al. 2025).

## 6. High-Leverage Leads & Open Questions

1. **Principled uncertainty inflation in $\mathcal{M}$-open worlds:** Generalized Bayes (Bissiri et al. 2016), SafeBayes (Grünwald & van Ommen 2017), and imprecise probabilities (Walley 1991) temper overconfidence, but calibrating *how much* to widen uncertainty (choosing $\eta$ or credal-set size) against *unseen* structural shifts without becoming vacuous remains open.
2. **Escaping the Forecaster's Dilemma on rare tails:** Combining threshold-weighted proper scores (Gneiting & Ranjan 2011), mechanistic severe testing (Mayo 2018), and sequential e-value monitoring (Ramdas et al. 2023) is a promising path to distinguish tail-risk competence from luck or alarmism.
3. **SAVI as an outer falsification shell:** Using game-theoretic e-processes (Ramdas et al. 2023) to continuously audit Bayesian or ML models online could operationalize Gelman & Shalizi's (2013) falsificationist loop in automated pipelines.
4. **Closing the ICP–DomainBed gap:** Characterizing the minimal interventional or multi-environment diversity required for invariant learning (Peters et al. 2016) to reliably beat ERM and shortcut learning (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz 2020/2021; Geirhos et al. 2020) is crucial for forecasting abrupt OOD collapse.
5. **Anti-laundering uncertainty interfaces:** Designing reporting standards that explicitly separate *in-model* parameter variance from *$\mathcal{M}$-open* sensitivity and predictive-check diagnostics would prevent downstream systems from treating narrow credible intervals as real-world safety proofs.
