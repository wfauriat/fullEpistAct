# Field 3: Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) & Robustness Analysis

Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) formalizes how ignorance, variability, and model imperfection propagate into predictions and decisions. Across engineering reliability, statistics, machine learning, and policy analysis, the central tension of modern UQ is a boundary problem: **nearly all tractable mathematical UQ machinery measures dispersion *within* an admitted model space $\Theta$, whereas catastrophic failures typically arise from mechanisms that lie *outside* $\Theta$.** This survey examines three foundational questions—the aleatory/epistemic split, structural ("unknown unknown") uncertainty, and the empirical visibility of omitted variables—separating settled results from active controversies and cataloging concrete methods alongside their theoretical limits.

---

## 1. Aleatory vs. Epistemic Uncertainty: Fundamental Property or Modeling Convenience?

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
In structural reliability, risk analysis, and machine learning, uncertainty is conventionally partitioned into two categories (Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen 2009; Hüllermeier & Waegeman 2021):
* **Aleatory (aleatoric) uncertainty:** Variability treated as irreducible randomness inherent to the data-generating process (e.g., measurement noise, turbulent fluctuations, stochastic outcomes). Gathering more data does not eliminate it, though it may refine estimates of its distribution.
* **Epistemic uncertainty:** Uncertainty arising from limited data, unknown model parameters, or imperfect structural knowledge. In principle, it is reducible through additional observations, experiments, or better theory.

Two practical results are broadly settled:
1. **Conflating epistemic and aleatory uncertainty distorts system reliability.** Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen (2009) demonstrated that the distinction matters mathematically—not merely philosophically—in multi-component and time-variant reliability problems. Epistemic uncertainties (such as an unknown shared model bias or common parameter error) are *shared across components or time steps*, inducing statistical dependence among failure events. Treating epistemic uncertainty as independent aleatory noise erases this dependence and systematically underestimates joint failure probabilities.
2. **Sources of uncertainty must be distinguished from types of uncertainty.** Hüllermeier & Waegeman (2021) emphasize that concrete *sources* (finite sample size, noisy labels, misspecified hypothesis classes) do not map one-to-one onto *types* (aleatoric vs. epistemic); how a source manifests depends on the representation and hypothesis space chosen by the modeler.

### (b) Actively Debated and Unresolved
* **Ontological Reality vs. Modeling Convenience:** Is the aleatory/epistemic divide a property of physical reality or strictly an artifact of the modeler's chosen resolution? Even Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen (2009) note that the boundary is pragmatic and historically mobile: what is modeled as "aleatory" randomness at a coarse scale (e.g., macroscopic wind gusts) becomes "epistemic" ignorance when finer mechanistic variables and sensors are introduced. Radical subjectivist Bayesians (cf. de Finetti 1974; Lindley 2006) go further, arguing that outside quantum indeterminacy, all probability is epistemic—representing a decision-maker's state of information—and that splitting probability into two "kinds" is ontologically incoherent. Reliability engineers and DMDU practitioners counter that even if the split is strictly operational, maintaining it is essential for resource allocation: epistemic uncertainty justifies investing in data collection or model refinement, whereas aleatory uncertainty justifies hedging, redundancy, or diversification.
* **Validity of Information-Theoretic Decompositions in Machine Learning:** In Bayesian deep learning and ensemble methods, it has become standard practice to decompose total predictive uncertainty (Shannon entropy of the predictive distribution) into aleatoric uncertainty (expected conditional entropy) plus epistemic uncertainty (mutual information between predictions and model parameters). This decomposition is now sharply contested. Wimmer et al. (2023) demonstrate critical mathematical and conceptual incoherencies in treating conditional entropy and mutual information as faithful measures of aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty—showing, for example, that the additive decomposition violates intuitive axioms under second-order uncertainty and depends heavily on arbitrary prior and architecture choices.

### (c) Concrete Frameworks, Tests, and Known Limitations
* **Second-Order / Nested Propagation:** Engineering UQ separates epistemic parameter draws (outer loop) from aleatory realizations (inner loop) to produce probability bounds or distributions over failure probabilities. *Limitation:* Computationally expensive in high dimensions and blind to structural misspecification outside the parameterized family.
* **Bayesian Neural Networks, Deep Ensembles, and Credal-Set Classifiers:** Machine learning operationalizes the split via posterior parameter variance, ensemble disagreement, or sets of probability distributions (credal sets; Hüllermeier & Waegeman 2021). *Limitation:* **Identifiability leakage**—when a model class is misspecified (structural epistemic error), the unexplained residuals are routinely absorbed into the estimated noise term, masquerading as irreducible aleatory variance and falsely signaling that additional modeling or data cannot help.

## 2. Structural & Ontological Uncertainty ("Unknown Unknowns")

By construction, variance decomposition, Bayesian posterior intervals, ensemble spread, and sensitivity analysis quantify disagreement *within* an admitted model space $\Theta$. When the true system lies outside $\Theta$ (an $\mathcal{M}$-open problem), all members of $\Theta$ can agree tightly on a wrong answer. Modern UQ has developed several distinct families of methods to confront structural and deep uncertainty, each exposing fundamental theoretical limits.

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
* **Levels of Uncertainty:** Policy and systems analysts broadly accept a graduated taxonomy of uncertainty (Walker et al. 2003; Marchau et al. 2019), ranging from shallow statistical uncertainty (well-characterized probability distributions) to **Level 4 / deep uncertainty**, where analysts cannot agree on the appropriate system model, the probability distributions over key inputs, or how to value outcomes.
* **Ignoring Model Discrepancy Biases Calibration:** In computer-model calibration, pretending a simulator is structurally exact forces parameter estimates to absorb structural error, producing biased parameters and severely overconfident predictions (Kennedy & O'Hagan 2001; Brynjarsdóttir & O'Hagan 2014).
* **Distribution-Free Marginal vs. Conditional Coverage:** Conformal prediction (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023) proves that finite-sample, distribution-free **marginal coverage**,
  $$\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{n+1} \in \widehat{C}_n(X_{n+1})\big) \ge 1 - \alpha,$$
  is achievable for *any* underlying black-box predictor and *any* data distribution, provided calibration and test data are **exchangeable**. Conversely, Barber, Candès, Ramdas, & Tibshirani (2019–2021) proved an exact **impossibility theorem** for distribution-free $X$-conditional coverage: without distributional assumptions, any method guaranteeing $\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1} \in \widehat{C}_n(X_{n+1}) \mid X_{n+1} = x) \ge 1-\alpha$ for all $x$ must produce infinitely wide (uninformative) prediction sets with high probability.

### (b) Actively Debated and Unresolved
* **The Discrepancy–Identifiability Trade-Off:** Kennedy & O'Hagan (2001) explicitly model structural inadequacy by adding a flexible Gaussian-process discrepancy term $\delta(x)$ to the physical simulator: $y(x) = \eta(x, \theta) + \delta(x) + \varepsilon$. While this protects against overconfidence inside the observed domain, it triggers a deep **identifiability crisis** (Brynjarsdóttir & O'Hagan 2014; Tuo & Wu): data alone cannot cleanly separate physical parameters $\theta$ from structural bias $\delta(x)$. A flexible $\delta(x)$ can compensate for physically nonsensical $\theta$, and when extrapolating into an unobserved regime, a Gaussian-process $\delta(x)$ reverts toward its prior mean—offering no genuine protection against novel mechanisms unless strong physical prior constraints are imposed on $\delta(x)$.
* **Local vs. Global Robustness (The Info-Gap Controversy):** Yakov Ben-Haim (1999, 2006, 2019) proposed **Info-Gap Decision Theory (IGDT)** as a non-probabilistic framework for severe uncertainty: rather than optimizing expected utility, one maximizes the "horizon of uncertainty" $\alpha$ over nested uncertainty sets $\mathcal{U}(\alpha, \tilde{u})$ around a nominal estimate $\tilde{u}$ within which a decision still meets a critical satisficing threshold. Mathematician Moshe Sniedovich (2007–2014) mounted a forceful, widely cited critique—dubbing IGDT a "voodoo decision theory"—by proving mathematically that IGDT's robustness function is a restricted reinvention of **Wald's maximin** (circa 1940) and classical **radius of stability** (circa 1960). Because IGDT expands nested sets strictly around a single nominal model $\tilde{u}$, Sniedovich argues it is inherently a **local robustness** measure: if $\tilde{u}$ is severely wrong (precisely the condition of deep uncertainty), maximizing local stability around $\tilde{u}$ can select catastrophically fragile policies. Defenders in the DMDU community still use IGDT for structured sensitivity trade-offs, but its claim to solve global/severe uncertainty remains heavily contested.
* **Imprecise Probabilities, Dilation, and Artificial Precision:** Frameworks such as Peter Walley's (1991) behavioral theory of **imprecise probabilities** (lower/upper previsions and credal sets), **Dempster–Shafer Theory (DST)** of belief functions, and Lotfi Zadeh's fuzzy/possibility theories reject the Bayesian requirement of a single precise probability measure under severe ignorance. Critics argue these frameworks suffer from computational intractability, decision indeterminacy, non-additive conditioning paradoxes, and **dilation** (studied by Walley and Seidenfeld): observing new data can *widen* the probability interval for an event regardless of which outcome is observed, even asymptotically. Proponents counter that dilation is not a mathematical flaw, but an honest warning that the data-generating dependence structure is itself ambiguous—whereas precise Bayesian priors conceal that ambiguity behind "artificial precision."

### (c) Concrete Methods and Their Theoretical Limits

| Method / Framework | Core Mechanism for Structural Uncertainty | Key References | Fundamental Theoretical / Practical Limit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Conformal Prediction & Extensions** | Distribution-free calibration of nonconformity scores; weighted or online adaptive updates under shift. | Vovk et al. (2005); Tibshirani et al. (2019); Gibbs & Candès (2021); Angelopoulos & Bates (2023); Barber et al. (2023) | Requires exchangeability or known/bounded drift; exact conditional coverage is impossible distribution-free; online adaptive methods recover long-run average coverage only *after* suffering unguaranteed transient misses during sudden regime breaks. |
| **Bayesian Model Discrepancy (KOH)** | Explicit stochastic discrepancy term $\delta(x)$ separating simulator $\eta(x,\theta)$ from reality. | Kennedy & O'Hagan (2001); Brynjarsdóttir & O'Hagan (2014); Tuo & Wu | Severe parameter–discrepancy confounding (unidentifiability); extrapolation of $\delta(x)$ collapses back to prior assumptions outside observed support. |

| **Info-Gap Decision Theory (IGDT)** | Satisficing robustness: maximize uncertainty horizon $\alpha$ around nominal model $\tilde{u}$ before failure. | Ben-Haim (1999, 2006, 2019); critique: Sniedovich (2007–2014) | Strictly *local* robustness anchored to $\tilde{u}$ (equivalent to Wald maximin / radius of stability); vulnerable when true dynamics lie far from $\tilde{u}$ or outside the parameterized uncertainty family. |

**Bottom line on Section 2:** No mathematical UQ framework can conjure guarantees against truly ontological "unknown unknowns" out of historical data alone. Every method trades an explicit structural assumption for a scoped guarantee: conformal prediction assumes exchangeability (or bounded drift); KOH assumes smoothness/priors on $\delta(x)$; IGDT anchors to a nominal model; RDM explores within a specified scenario generator. Where they succeed is in *widening* the boundary of stress-testing and *preventing false precision*—not in eliminating the outside of $\Theta$.

## 3. Empirical Visibility of Omitted Variables

A central question for empirical UQ is when an omitted mechanism $Z$ announces itself in historical data versus when it stays silent until failure.

### (a) What Is Broadly Settled
Consider a true system $Y = f(X, Z) + \varepsilon$ modeled solely via $\hat{f}(X)$. Standard statistical and econometric theory establishes clear conditions governing the visibility of $Z$:
* **When $Z$ manifests as elevated noise or diagnostic anomalies:** If $Z$ varies meaningfully across the historical sample, is not perfectly collinear with $X$, and actively influences $Y$, its omission leaves empirical fingerprints: inflated residual variance (absorbed as apparent aleatory noise), heteroskedasticity (if $Z$ interacts with $X$), serial autocorrelation (if $Z$ evolves slowly over time), or heavy-tailed residuals (if $Z$ experiences intermittent shocks). Under these conditions, residual diagnostics, cross-validation, and conformal prediction *will* widen uncertainty intervals—even without knowing what $Z$ is.
* **When $Z$ remains completely invisible in historical data:** Omitted variables evade *all* historical variance-based diagnostics under three structural regimes:
  1. **Regime-locked constants:** $Z$ remained constant (or nearly constant) throughout the observation window ($Z_t \approx z_0$). Because $\mathrm{Var}(Z) \approx 0$ in-sample, $Z$ contributes zero variance to historical residuals; its effect is silently folded into the model's intercept or calibrated parameters.
  2. **In-sample collinearity / shortcut confounding:** $Z$ varied historically, but moved in lockstep with an included feature $X$ ($Z \approx g(X)$). Predictive residuals stay small in-sample; the omission only becomes catastrophic when an intervention or shift breaks the $X$–$Z$ coupling.
  3. **Unactivated thresholds and tail-only interactions:** $Z$ enters through a nonlinear threshold, feedback loop, or tail-dependence mechanism that never crossed its activation boundary during the calibration period.

### (b) Actively Debated and Unresolved
* **Limits of Observational Early Warning:** Econometricians and UQ researchers debate how much can be inferred before a structural break occurs. While structural-break tests and online change-point detectors can flag *ongoing* drift, no purely observational test on a stationary historical sample can distinguish an invariant physical law from a contingent background condition that merely happened not to move. Competing schools disagree on whether the primary remedy is richer mechanistic/causal modeling (which risks over-parameterization and new blind spots) or institutional/adaptive safeguards that assume periodic unpredicted breaks (e.g., adaptive pathways in DMDU, Marchau et al. 2019).
* **Mathematical Misspecification vs. Socio-Technical Lock-In (The Pre-2008 Mortgage Correlation Case):** The 2007–2008 collapse of Collateralized Debt Obligations (CDOs) illustrates how regime-locked omissions become systemic. David Li's (2000) **Gaussian copula** modeled joint mortgage default times via a single scalar correlation parameter $\rho$, calibrated on a benign historical window of rising housing prices. Structurally, the Gaussian copula possesses **zero asymptotic tail dependence**, assigning near-zero probability to simultaneous mass defaults. Macroeconomic housing-price declines, nationwide collateral correlation, and forced-liquidation feedback loops acted as omitted, regime-locked drivers: because nationwide housing prices had not fallen sharply in the calibration window, those systemic mechanisms left no trace in historical variance. Crucially, sociologists Donald MacKenzie & Taylor Spears (2014) showed that this failure cannot be reduced to quant ignorance alone: the Gaussian copula functioned as an organizational coordination and pricing infrastructure embedded in a "culture of no-arbitrage modeling." Even when individual analysts recognized its tail-dependence flaws, risk-control rules and cross-hedging requirements locked trading desks into the shared model, turning a regime-locked omission into a market-wide blind spot.

### (c) Concrete Solutions, Empirical Tests, and Known Limitations
* **Out-of-Regime Stress-Testing & Counterfactual Perturbation:** Systematically perturbing variables that were historically constant (e.g., nationwide housing prices, liquidity correlations, operating temperatures) via mechanistic models or exploratory RDM ensembles. *Limitation:* Requires domain imagination to identify *which* silent background constants could move; cannot cover unimagined mechanisms.
* **Leading Structural Indicators over Lagging Residual Variance:** Monitoring upstream system state variables (leverage ratios, concentration, coupling density) rather than relying on historical residual volatility, which stays deceptively calm right up to a regime break. *Limitation:* Prone to false alarms during long calm periods, creating organizational pressure to discount or disable the indicators.

## 4. Cross-Domain Interactions & Interfaces

* **Field 1 — Philosophy of Science, Epistemology & the Frame Problem:** Regime-locked omitted variables are the statistical counterpart of implicit *ceteris paribus* background conditions ($A \cap (\dots) \to B$) and the AI frame problem (McCarthy & Hayes 1969; Shanahan 2016). Wimsatt's (1981) philosophical robustness analysis and Mayo's (1996, 2018) severe testing provide conceptual foundations for RDM and stress-testing, while Oberkampf & Roy (2010) formalize the verification–validation boundary that separates numerical UQ (inside code/equations) from structural discrepancy (between model and reality).
* **Field 2 — Bayesian Statistics & Foundations of Probability:** Structural UQ directly intersects $\mathcal{M}$-open Bayesian inference. Subjective Bayesians (de Finetti 1974; Savage 1954; Lindley 2006) challenge both the aleatory/epistemic split and imprecise probabilities, whereas proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery 2007) share the same visibility limit as residual UQ: they cannot penalize a forecaster for an omitted regime-locked variable until the regime breaks.
* **Field 4 — Decision Theory:** Deep uncertainty, imprecise probabilities, and IGDT map directly onto ambiguity aversion (Ellsberg), minimax regret, and asymmetric one-shot decisions where the expected-utility threshold $p^* = \Delta U / (\Delta U + L)$ is too small to estimate reliably from data—raising the question of when safety margins represent disciplined robustness versus arbitrary ritual.
* **Field 5 — Risk Engineering, System Reliability & Safety Science:** Der Kiureghian & Ditlevsen's (2009) proof that shared epistemic uncertainty induces correlated failures provides the mathematical basis for common-cause failure in redundant systems (Knight & Leveson 1986). Similarly, MacKenzie & Spears's (2014) account of organizational copula lock-in parallels *interface laundering*, where model caveats and structural error bars are stripped away across institutional handoffs.
* **Field 6 — Software Engineering & Formal Verification:** Formal verification proves guarantees *inside* a formal specification $\Theta$, leaving specification validation open. Runtime monitors and Simplex architectures (Sha 2001) act as operational counterparts to adaptive conformal/DMDU safeguards—intervening when real-world behavior violates the assumptions under which UQ guarantees hold.
* **Field 7 — Machine Learning & LLM Behavior:** Ensemble disagreement and semantic entropy (Farquhar et al. 2024) capture within-model high-entropy uncertainty, but fail on consistent/systematic errors and "jagged frontiers" (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023; Kalai & Vempala 2024)—a direct ML instance of structural consensus outside $\Theta$, compounded by the decomposition flaws identified by Wimmer et al. (2023).
* **Field 8 — Information Economics & Collective Epistemology:** Shared-model monoculture (when producers and auditors rely on the same foundation model or pricing copula) converts individual epistemic blind spots into correlated systemic risk, just as standardized copula pricing did in 2008.
* **Field 9 — Human Factors, Oversight & AI-Assisted Research:** Wide conformal sets, imprecise probability intervals, and dilation create cognitive-friction and alert-fatigue trade-offs for human operators. Conversely, structured human practices like *pre-mortems* (Klein 2007) serve as a low-cost cognitive heuristic for surfacing regime-locked invisible constants before deployment.

## 5. High-Leverage Leads & Open Questions

1. **Detecting Identifiability Leakage in Learned Models:** Can we design practical diagnostic tests that distinguish genuine irreducible aleatory noise from structural epistemic bias that has been absorbed into a learned model's noise head or residual variance?
2. **Bridging Conformal Guarantees and Single-Shot High-Stakes Decisions:** Adaptive conformal methods (Gibbs & Candès 2021; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023) guarantee long-run coverage under distribution shift, but permit transient miscoverage during abrupt regime breaks. What hybrid architectures (e.g., coupling conformal monitors with physical/formal Simplex fallbacks or conservative imprecise bounds) bound *single-shot* tail risk?
3. **Operationalizing "Global" vs. "Local" Robustness:** Given Sniedovich's (2007–2014) critique of Info-Gap as local robustness and RDM's dependence on its scenario generator, what formal or empirical criteria can measure whether a robustness analysis is genuinely exploring structural alternatives versus perturbing locally around a shared blind spot?
4. **Systematic Discovery of Invisible Constants:** Beyond ad hoc domain brainstorming and pre-mortems, can causal-graph elicitation, adversarial red-teaming, or cross-disciplinary model comparison systematically inventory which held-constant background variables are most likely to become active under deployment or scale-up?
5. **Institutional Incentives for Honest Uncertainty:** Following MacKenzie & Spears (2014), why do competitive markets and engineering bureaucracies systematically penalize models that express wider epistemic or imprecise bounds during calm regimes—and what governance mechanisms preserve honest uncertainty propagation across organizational interfaces?
