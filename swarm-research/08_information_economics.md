# Field 8: Information Economics & Collective Epistemology

Information economics and collective epistemology examine how markets, scientific communities, and technical organizations separate reliable signals from noise when verification is costly and actors hold asymmetric information. Generative AI alters the fundamental cost structure of these institutions: it drives the marginal cost of producing syntactically polished, plausible-looking artifacts toward zero while leaving the cost of substantive verification—empirical testing, formal proof, domain audit, or real-world execution—largely unchanged. Simultaneously, concentration around a small number of foundation models couples previously independent producers and evaluators.

This survey reviews three core problem areas—the collapse of costly signaling, shared-model monoculture, and evaluation bottlenecks—through an open, non-presupposing lens, separating settled results from active debates and evaluating proposed institutional and technical remedies alongside their known failure modes.

## 1. Collapse of Costly Signaling and Credible Provenance

### 1.1 What Is Broadly Settled

* **The single-crossing condition:** In Michael Spence’s (1973) signaling model, an observable trait (polished prose, clean syntax, thorough formatting) separates high-competence from low-type actors *only* when the marginal cost of producing the signal is strictly lower for the high type. When form is costly to fake, observers infer substance from surface polish.
* **Adverse selection and pooling:** In George Akerlof’s (1970) "market for lemons," when buyers cannot verify quality ex ante and sellers cannot credibly signal it, prices reflect average quality, driving high-quality suppliers out and collapsing the market toward a low-quality pooling equilibrium.
* **Erosion of form-based proxies:** When generative AI drives the marginal cost of fluent reports, proposals, code patches, and vulnerability disclosures toward zero for *all* types, single-crossing breaks down. Recent economic modeling (Bauer et al., 2025/2026, *The Last Costly Signal*) formalizes this: below a critical threshold of discernible headroom between human expert and machine output, production-side signaling fails and expert markets drift toward pooling unless alternative separating mechanisms exist.
* **Provenance is not truth:** Provenance metadata—recording *who* signed an artifact or *which tool* generated it—does not establish factual validity, logical soundness, or safety.

### 1.2 What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **Does cheap polish cause net welfare collapse or remove an unfair barrier?** A major counterargument to the "signaling collapse" thesis is that polished English prose and rigid formatting were always *noisy, socially biased* proxies for competence. Lowering the cost of linguistic polish can compress productivity disparities, raise baseline clarity, and level the playing field for non-native English speakers or under-resourced domain experts whose substantive ideas were previously discounted due to surface phrasing. Conversely, empirical work on academic peer review and hiring (e.g., Liang et al., 2024) reveals a perverse double bind: when institutions deploy AI-text detectors to restore screening, detectors disproportionately misclassify non-native English writing as AI-generated, penalizing non-native speakers whether they write unaided or use LLMs to polish their work. Whether generative AI produces net epistemic gain (better baseline expression, broader inclusion) or net loss (pooling collapse and defensive exclusion) remains domain-dependent and empirically contested.
* **Will new production-side signals emerge, or must signaling shift entirely to outcomes?** One school argues that markets will adapt by shifting from *static* artifact signals to *interactive* or *process* signals (e.g., live oral defense, commit history, interactive reproduction). Another school (e.g., Bauer et al., 2025/2026) contends that as agents automate process traces as well, only **outcome-contingent mechanisms**—legally enforceable liability, warranties, posted bonds, or reputational staking—can sustain separating equilibria in expert markets.
* **Equilibrium under partial observability:** Real markets rarely jump instantly to pure pooling; instead, they bifurcate into high-trust closed networks (relying on pre-existing relationships) and degraded open markets. How severe this bifurcation is across software, academia, journalism, and consulting—and how much talent is locked out of closed networks—remains an open empirical question.

### 1.3 Concrete Mechanisms, Empirical Tests, and Known Limitations

| Mechanism | How It Restores Separation | Known Limitations & Failure Modes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cryptographic provenance (C2PA)** | Embeds X.509-signed manifests (hard bindings via CBOR/COSE) recording creation tools and edit history (aligned with mandates like EU AI Act Art. 50). | **Metadata stripping:** Social platforms and CDNs routinely strip EXIF/XMP/manifest containers. **Certificate fragility:** Key compromise and revocation-check races degrade trust. **Scope gap:** Proves origin, not factual truth; breaks down on compound human-AI text documents. |
| **Statistical watermarking (e.g., SynthID)** | Embeds imperceptible soft bindings in pixel, audio, or token distributions to survive compression and link back to provenance records. | **Not cryptographic proof:** Acts only as a recovery lead; cannot guarantee absence of semantic tampering. **Adaptive attacks:** Vulnerable to paraphrasing (especially for text), screen-capture, and "generative laundering" (re-passing media through another model). |
| **Reproducible artifacts & executable checks** | Shifts signaling from prose to runnable code, deterministic builds, formal proofs, or verifiable test suites. | Only applies where ground-truth oracles exist; vulnerable to reward hacking (e.g., trivial tests passing buggy code) and still requires human validation that the test matches the intended specification. |
| **Outcome-contingent bonding, warranties & liability** | High-competence actors accept financial or legal penalties if downstream outcomes fail, restoring single-crossing because low-competence actors face higher expected liability. | Requires verifiable, attributable outcomes within a reasonable time horizon; fails for long-latency, noisy, or collectively determined outcomes (e.g., basic research, strategic advice, rare tail risks). |
| **Reputation & web-of-trust vouching** | Ties submissions to persistent identities with staked social capital. | Creates high barriers to entry for newcomers, entrenches incumbents, and is vulnerable to Sybil attacks or slow reputation harvesting followed by exit scams. |

## 2. Shared-Model Monoculture and Correlated Errors

### 2.1 What Is Broadly Settled

* **Monoculture harms welfare even without external shocks:** Jon Kleinberg and Manish Raghavan (2021, *PNAS*, "Algorithmic Monoculture and Social Welfare") proved that when multiple decision-makers abandon independent, noisier evaluations to adopt the exact same higher-accuracy ranking algorithm, aggregate social welfare can *decrease* even under normal operating conditions. Because all actors impose the same ordering, the system loses compensatory diversity (the "wisdom of crowds" and multi-path matching), systematically locking out candidates who are misranked by the shared model.
* **Foundation models generalize monoculture via shared components:** Rishi Bommasani et al. (Stanford CRFM / NeurIPS, "Picking on the Same Person: Does Algorithmic Monoculture Lead to Outcome Homogenization?") demonstrated that monoculture does not require identical end-user software. Building diverse downstream tools atop a tiny handful of shared foundation models and overlapping pre-training corpora produces **outcome homogenization**, where the same individuals or edge cases experience correlated failures across ostensibly independent institutions.
* **Producer–reviewer correlation and self-preference bias:** When LLMs act as automated reviewers ("LLM-as-a-judge") for outputs generated by themselves or closely related models, evaluation suffers from **self-preference bias** (also termed self-enhancement or model-family bias). Wataoka et al. (2024/2025) linked this bias directly to **perplexity**: judges assign disproportionately higher scores to outputs that have lower perplexity under their own probability distributions—rewarding familiarity and shared stylistic priors rather than objective human quality. Because producer and judge share weights, training data, or post-training alignment recipes, the judge systematically fails to catch the producer's errors.

### 2.2 What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **The individual-accuracy vs. correlation trade-off (when does shared AI help vs. hurt?):** Let a producer have marginal error rate $p$ and a reviewer have marginal false-negative (miss) rate $q$. Under independent checking, the system escape probability is $p \cdot q$. Under shared-model assistance, error events become positively correlated ($\rho > 0$), yielding an escape probability of:
  $$P(\text{escape}) = p_{\text{AI}} q_{\text{AI}} + \rho \sqrt{p_{\text{AI}}(1 - p_{\text{AI}}) q_{\text{AI}}(1 - q_{\text{AI}})}$$
  Shared AI reduces *mean* system risk when the reduction in marginal error rates ($p_{\text{AI}} \ll p$, $q_{\text{AI}} \ll q$) outweighs the correlation penalty $\rho$. However, for **tail risks**—rare, high-consequence, out-of-distribution edge cases where both models inherit the exact same blind spot ($\rho \to 1$)—shared assistance can *increase* systemic failure probability even while lowering routine error rates. Precisely characterizing where real-world domains sit on this trade-off curve (and whether routine gains justify correlated tail exposure) is hotly debated.
* **Does cross-family ensembling actually restore independence?** A common engineering proposal is to pair a producer from one vendor (e.g., Model Family A) with a judge from another (Model Family B). Recent 2025–2026 evaluations challenge whether this achieves meaningful epistemic independence: frontier models train on heavily overlapping web corpora, share synthetic distillation pipelines, and converge on similar RLHF/DPO alignment preferences. Distinguishing *true quality* from *shared-family bias* is itself methodologically difficult—prompting newer metrics such as the **DBG score** (*Beyond the Surface: Measuring Self-Preference in LLM Judgments*, EMNLP 2025), which uses gold human judgments as structural anchors to separate genuine model superiority from correlated self-preference.
* **Homogenization of scientific and cultural exploration:** Beyond error correlation, collective epistemologists debate whether shared foundation models narrow the *search space* of hypotheses, code architectures, and research designs. Even if individual outputs improve in average quality, collective discovery may suffer if independent teams converge on the same local optima—a tension between individual exploitation and collective exploration.

### 2.3 Concrete Solutions, Frameworks, and Known Limitations

* **Programmatic and non-neural verifiers:** Breaking producer–reviewer correlation works best when the verifier relies on a fundamentally different epistemic mechanism—such as compilers, static analyzers, formal proof checkers, unit/property-based tests, or physical experiments—rather than another neural language model. *Limitation:* Programmatic verifiers only cover formalizable properties; semantic validity, framing errors, and unspecified requirements remain unchecked.
* **Cross-family ensembles with independence auditing:** Using distinct model architectures/families as judges and calibrating them against blinded human-in-the-loop anchor sets can detect and partially discount self-preference bias. *Limitation:* Shared web training data and synthetic data contamination impose a hard floor on inter-model error correlation.
* **Structural separation of information channels:** In high-stakes audits, withholding the producer's reasoning trace or stylistic wrapper—forcing the reviewer to re-derive key quantities from raw inputs or independent sources—reduces anchoring and perplexity-driven agreement. *Limitation:* Increases review latency and cognitive cost, re-triggering evaluation bottlenecks.

## 3. Evaluation Bottlenecks and Institutional Governance

### 3.1 What Is Broadly Settled

* **Asymmetric scaling creates an "attention DDoS":** When generative tools scale output volume cheaply while independent human, peer-review, or experimental verification capacity remains roughly fixed, institutions face a structural **evaluation bottleneck**. Contributions that are privately cheap to generate impose large negative externalities on shared review infrastructure—a classic tragedy of the commons.
* **Empirical stress across software and science:** Real-world ecosystems have already crossed congestion thresholds:
  * *Open-source software:* High-profile maintainers experienced surges of plausible-looking but flawed automated contributions ("AI slop"). *curl* shuttered its HackerOne bug bounty program after being flooded with hallucinated vulnerability reports; *tldraw* temporarily auto-closed external pull requests; and empirical analyses of millions of GitHub PRs document surging submission volume paired with declining merge rates, particularly for first-time contributors.
  * *Academic peer review:* Corpus-level tracking by Liang et al. (2024) documented substantial LLM footprints in peer reviews at major machine learning conferences (ICLR, ICML), showing that evaluation bottlenecks induce *reviewers* themselves to outsource judgment to the same generative models, closing a low-effort feedback loop on both sides of the market.
* **Detection-based policing is brittle:** Relying on automated AI-text or AI-code detectors to gate submissions fails under adversarial evasion and generates unacceptable false-positive rates (especially against non-native writers), pushing organizations away from *origin policing* toward *behavioral and structural governance*.

### 3.2 What Is Actively Debated or Unresolved

* **Open participation vs. defensive exclusion:** Institutional responses to evaluation bottlenecks—such as rate limits, vouch lists, and contributor-only defaults—protect maintainer bandwidth but risk converting open epistemic commons (open-source software, open peer review, preprint servers) into closed guilds. Critics argue this recreates gatekeeping that disproportionately excludes newcomers and outsider talent; defenders argue that without friction, maintainer burnout collapses the commons entirely.
* **Can automated triage solve the bottleneck without introducing monoculture?** Deploying AI triage agents or automated review filters (e.g., anti-slop GitHub Actions) restores throughput, but reintroduces Field 8's second problem: if automated filters share the blind spots of automated generators, novel but unconventional human contributions may be filtered out while well-formatted synthetic errors pass through.
* **Optimal allocation of scarce human verification:** How should organizations decide *which* artifacts receive expensive human/experimental verification? Risk-based sampling, bounty-backed adversarial auditing, and tiered verification badges all compete, with little consensus on optimal allocation rules when base rates of subtle defects are unknown and shifting.

### 3.3 Institutional Governance Structures and Known Limitations

| Governance Mechanism | Operational Design & Real-World Examples | Known Limitations & Trade-Offs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Throughput caps & rate limiting** | Native platform controls (e.g., GitHub PR caps, per-user velocity limits) restrict open submissions per time window, acting as a circuit breaker against mass-automated submissions. | Penalizes legitimate high-output contributors during bursts; easily bypassed via multiple accounts unless paired with identity verification. |
| **Access gating & web-of-trust vouching** | Requires existing trusted collaborators to vouch for newcomers (e.g., *Ghostty*) or enforces mandatory issue linkage prior to submitting code. | Slows onboarding, raises barriers to entry, and concentrates power in incumbent social networks. |
| **Comprehension & intent verification ("Explain, don't just submit")** | Rewrites `CONTRIBUTING.md` so humans bear full responsibility; requires authors to explain design trade-offs or reproduction steps in their own words, closing submissions that fail basic comprehension checks without needing to prove AI use. | Still consumes triage attention to evaluate explanations; increasingly vulnerable as interactive agents coach users through follow-up questions. |
| **Automated defensive layering** | Repository-level instruction files (`AGENTS.md` / `CLAUDE.md`), strict linters, and CI gates reject low-effort automated outputs before they reach human queues. | Only enforces syntactic/local rules; cannot detect subtle architectural or semantic flaws; creates an arms race with submission agents. |
| **Verification-status tagging & tiered provenance** | Explicitly labels artifacts by verification tier (e.g., *unverified draft*, *CI-tested*, *independently reproduced*, *formally verified*) rather than treating all published outputs as epistemically equal. | Prone to "interface laundering" downstream: consumers routinely strip or ignore verification tags and treat unverified drafts as authoritative. |
| **Pre-registered expectations & outcome ledgers** | Requires producers or reviewers to log falsifiable predictions, expected magnitudes, or test criteria *before* observing outputs, and tracks longitudinal accuracy in persistent outcome ledgers. | High upfront friction; vulnerable to Goodharting on easily measurable metrics while neglecting unlogged qualitative risks. |

## 4. Cross-Domain Interactions & Interfaces

Field 8 sits at the institutional and multi-agent layer of the broader nine-field architecture, translating individual epistemic and technical limits into systemic market and organizational dynamics:

* **Philosophy of Science & Epistemology (Field 1):** Shared-model monoculture is a collective *frame problem* (McCarthy & Hayes 1969; Dennett 1984; Shanahan 2016): shared implicit background assumptions make self-auditing circular. Breaking monoculture parallels Deborah Mayo’s *severe testing* (1996, 2018) and William Wimsatt’s *robustness analysis* (1981)—requiring genuinely independent lines of evidence—while *verification-status tagging* operationalizes Oberkampf & Roy’s (2010) verification–validation split.
* **Bayesian Statistics & Probability (Field 2):** Outcome ledgers and bonding rely on *strictly proper scoring rules* (Gneiting & Raftery 2007), but inherit their limitation: when outcomes are rare, delayed, or non-stationary, scoring rules separate types too slowly to prevent short-run pooling. Shared foundation models also act as an unacknowledged common prior in $\mathcal{M}$-open settings, masking misspecification behind inter-model agreement.
* **Uncertainty Quantification & Robustness (Field 3):** Ensemble spread across correlated foundation models underestimates epistemic and structural uncertainty within the admitted model space $\Theta$. *Robust Decision Making* (Lempert, Popper & Bankes 2003) and *conformal prediction* (Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023) bound risk under bottlenecks, though neither detects mechanisms omitted from calibration data.
* **Decision Theory (Field 4):** In asymmetric one-shot decisions where $L \gg \Delta U$, the threshold $p^* = \Delta U / (\Delta U + L) \to 0$. Correlated tail risk under monoculture can silently push true failure probability above $p^*$ despite low routine error rates, motivating hard operational locks or liability regimes.
* **Risk Engineering & Safety Science (Field 5):** Monoculture parallels *common-cause failure* in N-version software (Knight & Leveson 1986), while cross-family review faces the *independence–comparability trade-off*. Evaluation bottlenecks trigger *alert fatigue* (Bliss, Gilson & Deaton 1995) as low-quality automated submissions depress positive predictive value, and *interface laundering* strips verification tags across handoffs.
* **Software Engineering & Formal Verification (Field 6):** Executable contracts, runtime monitors (Sha 2001), and autoformalization in Lean (Moura & Ullrich 2021; Wu et al. 2022) relieve evaluation bottlenecks for *verifiable* properties, shifting scarce human effort from checking derivations to validating specifications.
* **Machine Learning & LLM Behavior (Field 7):** *Jagged frontiers* (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023) decouple fluency from reliability; *unavoidable hallucinations* (Kalai & Vempala 2024; Kalai et al. 2025) and *sycophancy* / Goodhart effects (Goodhart 1975; Strathern 1997; Sharma et al. 2024) amplify producer–reviewer correlation; and *model collapse* (Shumailov et al. 2024) captures unverified synthetic outputs re-entering collective corpora.
* **Human Factors & AI-Assisted Research (Field 9):** Fluent AI outputs suppress *uncertainty cues* (rough drafts, hedging) that guide human verification, compounding *ironies of automation* (Bainbridge 1983) and miscalibrated speed perceptions (Becker et al. 2025). Cognitive protocols—*pre-mortems* (Klein 2007), pre-registering expected magnitudes, and retrieval-grounded verification (Lewis et al. 2020)—mirror institutional comprehension checks and outcome ledgers.

## 5. High-Leverage Leads & Open Questions

1. **Quantifying the monoculture tipping point empirically:** Under what concrete empirical regimes (code security, clinical triage, scientific peer review) does the correlation penalty $\rho$ of shared foundation models outweigh their reduction in marginal error rates $p_{\text{AI}}, q_{\text{AI}}$? Developing empirical fault-injection benchmarks (analogous to mutation testing, DeMillo, Lipton & Sayward 1978) to measure *correlated escape probability* across model families is a high-leverage research target.
2. **Designing low-friction, non-exclusionary separating equilibria:** How can open-source and scientific institutions filter synthetic noise without collapsing into closed incumbent guilds or penalizing non-native speakers? Testing hybrid mechanisms—such as refundable micro-bonds, progressive trust tiers, and standardized comprehension protocols—in field experiments could clarify the openness–verification trade-off.
3. **Resilient verification-status propagation:** Given that C2PA metadata is routinely stripped and verification tags are laundered across organizational interfaces, what technical and institutional designs keep *verification provenance* (not merely *generation provenance*) attached to claims as they move from drafts to decisions?
4. **Measuring true epistemic diversity across foundation models:** With widespread pre-training data overlap and synthetic distillation, vendor diversity no longer guarantees error independence. What operational metrics (extending frameworks like the EMNLP 2025 DBG score) can audit *effective independence* between a producer and an automated reviewer before deploying them in a closed loop?
5. **Liability and insurance as epistemic infrastructure:** If outcome-contingent liability (Bauer et al., 2025/2026) is required to restore separating equilibria in expert markets, how do insurers, regulators, and courts price and verify AI-assisted epistemic risk when causal attribution across human–AI workflows is diffuse?
